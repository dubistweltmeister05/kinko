So I work this job that hires me as a founding firmware engineer. 

And the work within that title comes a lot more than just "Firmware". I may have not wanted a lot of it, and I cannot say that I particularly like much of what comes with my role, but I do it to the best of my abilities, and all in the hopes that it helps me grow as a person first and then an engineer. 

Sounds like I am complaining? Hell yea. But does the job still get done? Yes. So you may say to yourself, this dude is fucking contradicting himself after each sentence, I should just scroll away. Maybe you should. But I have had a train of thought here, so if you like to read thought dumps, here. 

Look man, AI for majority off the developers was like the meme about giving a monkey an AK. You know the one. And those of you, who work in corporate, can relate to me when I say that "Owning" code is more like a suggestion rather than a rule. We have traded understanding for speed of building things, and in a massive environment like that of a conglomerate, there will be YEARS till you see the ramifications of you pushing code to meet your KPIs without understanding the very thing you are committing. You have your skill files and your agents and your guardrails, and so on and so forth, but you know you have pushed code multiple times without giving a damn about what's being generated, as long as all the test cases in the testing suite pass without failure. 

You end up vibing to a blackbox generated by the LLM, and you end up being a slave to it's behavior. In a startup, however, you cannot simply hide behind the bureaucracy and the middle management, and the layers of abstraction that lie between you and accountability.  You commit something without understanding it end-to-end, your goose is cooked faster than you can take a sigh of relief. So - working with firmware here, taught me how NOT to trade understanding, design, thought, and responsibility to a clanker. And how to use it to ship faster, but ship better and more mindfully at the same time. 

Now, nothing from me is committed without a document that holds my own understanding about the module that I have worked on, the feature that I have written, the design behind it, the errors that I faced and debugged, and a lot more - of course, to varying degrees of detail. Hell, I spend my time back home writing these, for the sole reason that it helps me learn and understand the code generated by the LLM for the system that I thought about. To see something come to life, and work as it should, is a blessing in disguise, but understanding the intricacies that lie under the hood of it all, is nothing but pure joy for me man.
